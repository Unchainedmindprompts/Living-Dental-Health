*By [Dr. Andy Engel](/about), Living Dental Health, Bend, Oregon.*

You trade soda for a sugar-free sparkling water. It feels like a sensible change. But then you hear that bubbly water might be bad for your teeth. Do you really need to give it up?

**Not necessarily. Plain sparkling water and acid-flavored sparkling drinks are not all alike. What is in the drink—and how often it washes over your teeth—matters.**

In our office, conversations about changing drink habits increasingly include bubbly water. Some patients enjoy several a day; others keep one nearby and sip for hours. Those conversations are a useful starting point for prevention, but they do not prove that sparkling water caused a patient's cavities.

## First, separate erosion from cavities

Enamel is the hard outer surface of a tooth. Dietary acids can dissolve its minerals directly, a process called **dental erosion**. The [American Dental Association explains that erosion does not involve acids produced by bacteria](https://www.ada.org/resources/ada-library/oral-health-topics/dental-erosion).

Cavities develop differently. Bacteria use sugars and starches to produce acids that attack teeth. The [National Institute of Dental and Craniofacial Research describes this process](https://www.nidcr.nih.gov/health-info/tooth-decay), including how early mineral loss can sometimes be reversed before a cavity forms.

That distinction matters: removing sugar is helpful, but a sugar-free drink can still be acidic.

## What the research says about bubbly water

Carbon dioxide dissolved in water forms carbonic acid. That makes plain carbonated water mildly acidic, but acidity alone does not tell the whole story.

In a [2001 laboratory study of mineral waters](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11556958/), still and sparkling mineral waters caused very little mineral dissolution compared with the soft drinks tested. Sparkling waters produced slightly more dissolution than still waters, but the results did not support treating carbonation alone as a major erosion concern.

A [2007 laboratory study of flavored sparkling waters](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17263857/) found a different pattern: the products tested had appreciable acidity and could erode enamel under the study conditions. Added fruit acids were important contributors.

These were laboratory experiments, not long-term trials measuring cavities in people. They help explain why formulations matter; they do not establish that every flavored water is equally erosive or that a particular number of cans will damage your teeth.

### Look beyond the flavor name

Check the ingredient list for added acids, such as citric acid, and for sugar. A citrus flavor name alone does not tell you a drink's exact composition. Recipes vary between products, and a pH measurement for one formulation should not be applied to an entire brand.

## Why a pH number is not a safety score

The question patients often bring is simple: what pH is safe? In practice, there is no single number that predicts what a beverage will do in your mouth.

A drink's pH describes acidity at the time of measurement. Mineral content, the amount of acid that must be neutralized, saliva, and contact time also influence its effects. The mineral-water and flavored-water studies above illustrate why two acidic drinks can behave differently.

Home pH strips may satisfy your curiosity, but they cannot diagnose erosion or certify a drink as safe for all-day sipping. Your dentist can assess actual changes in your teeth and discuss your habits with you.

## Small habits that make a difference

The [ADA's prevention guidance](https://www.ada.org/resources/ada-library/oral-health-topics/dental-erosion) supports reducing repeated acid exposure and rinsing with water afterward. Start here:

- **Have acidic drinks with a meal.** Avoid stretching one drink into hours of repeated sipping.
- **Swallow normally.** Do not hold or swish the drink around your teeth.
- **Follow with plain water.** A rinse helps clear the drink from your mouth.
- **Give brushing some space.** Avoid brushing immediately after acidic drinks. [Mayo Clinic recommends waiting an hour](https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/adult-health/expert-answers/brushing-your-teeth/faq-20058193).
- **Keep plain still water nearby.** It is a straightforward choice for routine hydration between meals.

Saliva helps clear and buffer acids. There is no exact recovery time that applies to every person or every drink, particularly when someone has a dry mouth.

## Where fluoride fits

Brush twice daily with fluoride toothpaste and clean between your teeth. [NIDCR recommends fluoride for cavity prevention](https://www.nidcr.nih.gov/health-info/tooth-decay), including its role in helping repair early mineral loss.

Fluoride does not make repeated acid exposure harmless. Erosion prevention still depends on managing the source and frequency of acid. Early mineral changes and lost enamel are also different: enamel that has physically worn away does not grow back.

You do not need to feel guilty about enjoying something fizzy. Bring the ingredient label or a photo of your usual drink to your next visit. We can put it in context with your dental history, sensitivity, saliva, and everyday routine.
